## Without the skill



**Subject: Ninth Circuit sends a DEI-training hostile environment claim to a jury (Diemert v. City of Seattle)**

**To:** General Counsel
**Date:** October 14, 2026
**Source:** Parker Poe client alert, "Ninth Circuit allows anti-discrimination training bias claim to proceed to trial" (October 9, 2026)

---

**Bottom line**

The Ninth Circuit has reversed a summary judgment win for the City of Seattle. It held that a white male employee put forward enough evidence for a jury to decide whether a mandatory anti-discrimination class, and what followed it, created a hostile work environment based on race or sex. The court did not hold that DEI training is unlawful. It said such training is "not inherently discriminatory." The case matters because it breaks with a run of decisions that threw out similar claims as too subjective or speculative. For employees in the Ninth Circuit, the content of our training and how managers handle what comes after it now carry real litigation risk.

**What happened**

- **Claim.** The employee says the instructor called white people "devils" and said racism is built into their DNA. He says that after the class (1) co-workers repeatedly called him a racist and (2) he lost job opportunities that were reserved for persons of color.
- **Posture.** The district court granted summary judgment to the City. The Ninth Circuit reversed, so the hostile environment claim now goes to trial. These are allegations. No court or jury has found them true.
- **Reasoning, as the alert describes it.** DEI training is not discriminatory in itself, but these facts were enough for a jury to decide whether the class led to a hostile environment. The alert's authors think the "extreme and explosive" content alleged here probably drove the result. That is their reading, not something the court said.

**Why it matters to us**

1. **The defense playbook is weaker.** Employers have mostly won these cases early by arguing that offense taken at training content isn't severe or pervasive enough to count. In the Ninth Circuit, that argument may no longer end a case before trial when the content stereotypes a group and is followed by workplace conduct like co-worker name-calling.
2. **Liability comes from the content plus what follows.** The claim joins three things: what was taught, how co-workers treated the plaintiff afterward, and employment decisions. The risk is not only the slide deck. It is also whether managers let the training become a license to harass, and whether any opportunities are limited by race or sex.
3. **More scrutiny is coming.** The alert notes that the federal government is increasingly challenging DEI initiatives. It warns that employers who allow conduct like what's alleged here face both private suits and agency enforcement.

**Recommended actions**

1. **Review current training content and vendors.** Look closely at material that attributes inherent bias, racism, or other traits to any race or sex. Get scripts, decks, and facilitator guides from outside presenters, not just outlines. The alert's main advice is to vet both the presenters and the content.
2. **Look at who controls ad-libbing.** The alleged statements here were the instructor's. Vendor contracts and facilitator instructions should require presenters to stay within approved content, and someone should be able to monitor delivery.
3. **Check eligibility rules for opportunities.** The allegation of opportunities "reserved for persons of color" is the most legally exposed piece. Confirm that no program, assignment, promotion track, or development opportunity limits eligibility by race or sex.
4. **Treat training-related complaints like any other harassment complaint.** Make sure HR and employee relations know that complaints about training content, or about retaliation or name-calling after it, are covered by our anti-harassment and complaint procedures and must be investigated.
5. **Preserve and protect.** If any training-related complaints or demand letters are open now, revisit our early-dismissal strategy in light of this decision and confirm that litigation holds cover training materials.

**Limits of this briefing and open questions**

- **Single secondary source.** This is based only on a short law-firm alert. I have not reviewed the opinion. Before we rely on it, we need the citation, the decision date (the alert says only "last week," which puts it around the start of October 2026), whether the opinion is published and therefore precedential or unpublished, and the panel.
- **Scope of the holding.** The alert discusses only the hostile environment claim. It does not say whether other claims were brought or how they came out, such as disparate treatment over lost opportunities, equal protection or First Amendment theories (Seattle is a public employer), or state-law claims under Washington law. It also does not explain what standard the court applied to the co-worker conduct.
- **Public vs. private employer.** The defendant is a city. Most of the hostile environment analysis should carry over to private employers under Title VII, but some parts of a public-employer case may not.
- **Geography.** The ruling binds federal courts in the Ninth Circuit (Alaska, Arizona, California, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, Oregon, Washington). How much this affects us depends on our headcount in those states. Elsewhere it is persuasive at most, and it runs against the trend in other courts.
- **Further review.** We don't know whether the City will seek rehearing en banc or certiorari, and the case is now headed to trial. Both are worth watching.

**Suggested next step:** Ask outside employment counsel to pull and summarize the opinion, and have HR and L&D give us an inventory of current DEI and anti-discrimination training content and vendors for a privileged review.
